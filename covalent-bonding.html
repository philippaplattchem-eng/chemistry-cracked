🔂 1.4.1.1 What is covalent bonding? F + H ▼

A covalent bond forms when two non-metal atoms each share one of their electrons. The two shared electrons — one from each atom — are called a shared pair, and it is this shared pair that holds the atoms together.

The definition — get the exact wording right

Examiners are very specific. You must say pair. "Sharing electrons" on its own is not enough.

2 marks Define a covalent bond.

A shared pair of electrons (1) between two non-metal atoms / the electrostatic attraction between the shared electrons and the nuclei of both bonded atoms (1)

⚠️ Common error: Writing "electrons are shared between atoms" without saying pair loses the mark. The mark scheme specifically rejects "sharing electrons" alone. Always write shared pair.

How a covalent bond forms

Non-metal atoms need to fill their outer electron shell to become stable. Rather than gaining or losing electrons (like metals do), two non-metals each contribute one electron to a shared pair. Both atoms then "count" this shared pair as part of their own outer shell, so both achieve a full outer shell at the same time.

H₂: one shared pair of electrons in the overlap — both H atoms now have a full outer shell of 2

Single, double and triple bonds

Bond typeShared pairsExampleDrawn as
Single1 shared pair (2 electrons)H₂, HCl, H₂O, CH₄, NH₃H — H
Double2 shared pairs (4 electrons)O₂, CO₂O = O
Triple3 shared pairs (6 electrons)N₂N ≡ N

✅ Quick check

Which molecule contains a double covalent bond: H₂, HCl, O₂, or NH₃?

O₂ — oxygen forms a double bond (2 shared pairs) because each oxygen atom has 6 outer electrons and needs 2 more. H₂ has a single bond; HCl has a single bond; NH₃ has three single bonds.
🖉 1.4.2.1 Dot and cross diagrams F + H ▼

Draw two overlapping circles — one for each atom. Put one atom's electrons as dots (●) and the other atom's as crosses (×). The bonding pair goes in the overlap. Lone pairs sit outside the overlap on the atom that owns them.

How to draw any dot and cross diagram — 4 steps

  1. Work out how many outer electrons each atom has (group number = outer electrons for groups 1–7).
  2. Work out how many bonds each atom needs (electrons needed to fill its outer shell).
  3. Draw overlapping circles — one circle per atom. Put the element symbol inside.
  4. Place the bonding pair (one dot + one cross) in the overlap. Add lone pairs to the atom that owns them, outside the overlap. Show outer electrons only.
💡 Rule: The shared pair must have one dot and one cross — one electron from each atom. Using two dots or two crosses loses the mark unless you clearly label which symbol belongs to which atom.

The key diagrams

H₂ — single bond

1 shared pair. Each H has 1 outer electron; needs 1 more. Both get full outer shell of 2.

HCl — single bond + lone pairs

1 shared pair. Cl has 7 outer electrons; 3 lone pairs remain on Cl after bonding.

H₂O — two single bonds

O has 6 outer electrons; forms 2 bonds; 2 lone pairs remain on O.

CH₄ — four single bonds

C has 4 outer electrons; forms 4 bonds; no lone pairs on C.

NH₃ — three single bonds + lone pair

N has 5 outer electrons; forms 3 bonds; 1 lone pair remains on N.

O₂ — double bond

O needs 2 more electrons; forms a double bond (4 electrons in the overlap). 2 lone pairs on each O.

CO₂ — two double bonds

C forms 2 double bonds (one to each O). Each O has 2 lone pairs. C has no lone pairs.

✅ Quick check

Element X has electronic configuration 2.4. Element Y has electronic configuration 2.6. How many bonds does X form, and what is the formula of the compound X forms with Y?

X has 4 outer electrons → forms 4 bonds (8 − 4 = 4). Y has 6 outer electrons → forms 2 bonds (8 − 6 = 2). Each Y atom uses 2 of X's 4 bonds, so X needs 2 Y atoms → formula is XY₂. (Not XY₄ or X₂Y — check how many bonds each atom forms first.)
💧 1.5.1.1 / 1.5.2.1 Simple molecular substances F + H ▼

Simple molecular substances (like H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, O₂) are made of small separate molecules. The atoms inside each molecule are held together by strong covalent bonds — but the molecules themselves are only attracted to each other by weak intermolecular forces. It is these weak forces that determine their properties.

Strong covalent bonds hold atoms together inside each molecule. Weak intermolecular forces act between separate molecules.

Properties and why — the most important distinction

PropertyWhat it isWhy (the explanation)
Low melting and boiling pointsMany are gases or liquids at room temperatureOnly the weak intermolecular forces between molecules need to be overcome when melting or boiling. These need very little energy.
Do not conduct electricityIn any state — solid, liquid or dissolvedNo free ions and no free/delocalised electrons — there are no charged particles that can move to carry charge.
Larger molecule = higher boiling pointe.g. butane (−1°C) > methane (−162°C)Larger molecules have more electrons, so stronger intermolecular forces, requiring more energy to overcome.
⚠️ The single biggest mark-scheme trap: When explaining low boiling points or why a substance is a gas, you MUST write intermolecular forces. Writing "weak bonds" or "weak covalent bonds" is rejected. The covalent bonds inside the molecule are strong — they are NOT broken on melting or boiling.
2 marks Explain why methane is a gas at room temperature.

Methane has weak intermolecular forces between molecules. (1)

Very little energy is needed to overcome these, so the boiling point is below room temperature. (1)

Do NOT write: "weak bonds" / "weak covalent bonds" / "the bonds break easily" — these are all rejected.

2 marks Explain why simple covalent substances do not conduct electricity.

There are no free/mobile ions. (1)

There are no free/mobile electrons (electrons are fixed in covalent bonds / no delocalised electrons). (1)

Combined answer "no ions or free electrons" scores only 1 mark, not 2.

3 marks Explain why butane (C₄H₁₀) has a higher boiling point than methane (CH₄).

Butane is a larger molecule / has more electrons / has greater relative molecular mass than methane. (1)

Therefore butane has stronger intermolecular forces between its molecules. (1)

More energy is needed to overcome these intermolecular forces. (1)

💎 1.5.3.1 Diamond and graphite F + H ▼

Diamond and graphite are both made entirely of carbon atoms. The only difference is the arrangement of those atoms — and that single structural difference creates completely opposite properties.

Diamond: 3D giant covalent lattice. Each C bonds to 4 others.

Graphite: layers of hexagonal rings. Each C bonds to 3 others. Weak forces between layers.

Comparison table

FeatureDiamondGraphite

Bonds per C atom4 covalent bonds3 covalent bonds

Delocalised electrons?No — all 4 outer electrons used in bondsYes — 1 delocalised electron per C atom (4th electron not used in bonding)

⚠️ Common errors: (1) Writing "strong bonds between layers" in graphite — layers are held by weak intermolecular forces. (2) Forgetting that graphite also has a very high melting point — you must break the strong covalent bonds within the layers to melt it. (3) Saying diamond doesn't conduct because it "has no electrons" — it has electrons but none are free/delocalised.

Why diamond is hard and used in cutting tools

Each carbon atom is held in a giant 3D network by four strong covalent bonds pointing in different directions. To scratch or break diamond, you must break a huge number of these strong bonds simultaneously. This requires an enormous amount of energy — making diamond one of the hardest substances known and ideal for maintaining a sharp cutting edge.

Why graphite is soft and used as a lubricant

Within each layer, the carbon atoms are held by strong covalent bonds — but the layers are only attracted to each other by weak intermolecular forces. A small sideways force causes the layers to slide over each other easily. This makes graphite soft and slippery — perfect as a lubricant and why pencil "lead" leaves marks on paper.

Why graphite conducts electricity

Each carbon uses only 3 of its 4 outer electrons in covalent bonds. The 4th electron per carbon atom is delocalised — free to move through the layer. These mobile electrons carry electrical charge when a voltage is applied. This is why graphite is used as electrodes in electrolysis cells.

6 marks Compare diamond (scalpel blade) and graphite (pencil lead) — explain each use in terms of bonding and structure.

Diamond:

• Diamond has a giant covalent / giant lattice structure. (1)

• Each carbon atom is bonded to 4 other carbon atoms by strong covalent bonds. (1)

• Many strong covalent bonds need a large amount of energy to break, so diamond is very hard. (1)

• Therefore suitable for cutting / maintaining a sharp edge on a scalpel. (1)

Graphite:

• Graphite has a layered / hexagonal layer structure. (1)

• Each carbon atom is bonded to 3 other carbon atoms within the layer; layers are held together by weak intermolecular forces. (1)

• Layers can slide over each other easily, so graphite is soft and leaves marks on paper. (1)

Max 3 marks per substance. Must discuss both to score above 3.

✅ Quick check

Why does graphite conduct electricity but diamond does not?

In graphite, each carbon forms only 3 covalent bonds, leaving one delocalised electron per atom that is free to move and carry charge. In diamond, each carbon forms 4 bonds — all outer electrons are fixed in bonds, leaving no free electrons to carry charge.
📍 1.5.3.1 Silicon dioxide and giant covalent structures F + H ▼

Silicon dioxide (SiO₂) is sand. Like diamond, it is a giant covalent structure — every atom is bonded to all its neighbours by strong covalent bonds throughout the whole structure. This is why sand has a melting point of 1713°C, not −78°C like CO₂.

SiO₂: each silicon bonds to 4 oxygen atoms; each oxygen bridges two silicon atoms — a continuous 3D covalent network.

SiO₂ vs CO₂ — the classic comparison question

Both molecules contain only covalent bonds. Yet SiO₂ melts at 1713°C and CO₂ is a gas at room temperature. The reason is their type of structure, not just the bonds.

SiO₂ (silicon dioxide)

  • Giant covalent structure
  • Every Si and O atom bonded throughout the whole 3D lattice
  • To melt: must break many strong covalent bonds throughout the structure
  • Requires a huge amount of energy → very high melting point

CO₂ (carbon dioxide)

  • Simple molecular structure
  • Discrete CO₂ molecules — covalent bonds hold atoms within each molecule
  • To boil: only need to overcome weak intermolecular forces between molecules
  • Very little energy needed → gas at room temperature
3 marks Explain why SiO₂ has a much higher melting point than CO₂.

Silicon dioxide has a giant covalent structure; carbon dioxide is a simple molecular structure. (1)

SiO₂ has many strong covalent bonds throughout the lattice, requiring a large amount of energy to break. (1)

CO₂ has only weak intermolecular forces between molecules, requiring very little energy to overcome. (1)

Must contrast both structures for full marks. Writing "stronger bonds in SiO₂" without naming "giant covalent structure" is rejected.

Properties of all giant covalent structures

PropertyGiant covalentWhy
Melting pointVery highMany strong covalent bonds throughout — all must be broken to melt
Electrical conductivityDoes not conduct (usually)No free ions; no delocalised electrons (except graphite and graphene)
SolubilityInsolubleWater cannot pull apart the covalent network
HardnessUsually very hardRigid network of strong bonds (exception: graphite is soft)
✅ Quick check

A substance has a melting point of 1710°C, is insoluble in water, and does not conduct electricity in any state. What type of structure is it? Give a reason.

Giant covalent structure. Reason: very high melting point indicates many strong covalent bonds throughout the structure that require large amounts of energy to break; does not conduct because there are no free electrons or ions in any state.
⚽ 1.5.4.1 Fullerenes and graphene F + H ▼

Fullerenes (including C₆₀) and graphene are two more forms of carbon — both built from the same hexagonal rings as graphite, but arranged differently. Each carbon still forms 3 covalent bonds, leaving one delocalised electron per atom.

Buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀): 60 carbon atoms arranged as 20 hexagons + 12 pentagons — the same pattern as a football. Each C forms 3 covalent bonds; 1 electron per atom is delocalised.

The three forms

FormStructureBonds per CDelocalised e⁻?Key uses
Buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀)Hollow sphere — 60 C atoms, hexagons + pentagons3Yes — over the moleculeDrug delivery, lubricants, semiconductors
Carbon nanotubesHexagonal sheet rolled into a long hollow cylinder3Yes — along the tubeReinforcing materials, electronics, nanotechnology
GrapheneSingle layer of graphite — one atom thick, flat hexagonal sheet3Yes — across the sheetElectronics, sensors, composite materials

Key facts to remember

  • All three are allotropes of carbon — same element, different structure.
  • C₆₀ is a discrete molecule (not a giant structure) — C₆₀ molecules are held to each other by weak intermolecular forces, just like simple molecular substances.
  • Graphene is one atom thick — the thinnest possible layer of graphite.
  • All conduct electricity due to delocalised electrons.
  • Carbon nanotubes have extremely high tensile strength (stronger than steel by weight) — used to reinforce composites.
H Why C₆₀ has a lower melting point than diamond: C₆₀ molecules are discrete units held together only by weak intermolecular forces. In diamond, every atom is held by strong covalent bonds throughout the whole network. Melting C₆₀ only requires overcoming weak intermolecular forces; melting diamond requires breaking every C–C covalent bond.
✅ Quick check

Why does graphene conduct electricity?

Each carbon in graphene forms 3 covalent bonds, using only 3 of its 4 outer electrons. The 4th electron per atom is delocalised — not fixed to any one atom and free to move across the sheet. These mobile electrons carry electrical charge when a voltage is applied.
🌟 1.5.1.1 Types of substances and exam technique F + H ▼

There are four types of substance. Every classification question in the exam comes down to one rule: find the structure, then the properties follow automatically.

The four types — master comparison table

PropertyIonicSimple molecularGiant covalentMetallic
Melting pointHighLowVery highUsually high
Conducts as solid?No (ions fixed)NoNo (except graphite)Yes
Conducts when molten/dissolved?Yes (ions free)NoNoYes (molten)
Soluble in water?Many areMost are notNoNo
ExamplesNaCl, MgOH₂O, CO₂, CH₄Diamond, graphite, SiO₂Fe, Cu, Al

Classification flowchart — for data table questions

  1. Does it conduct as a solid? Yes → metallic (or graphite/graphene). No → not metallic. Move on.
  2. Does it conduct when molten or dissolved but not as a solid? Yes → ionic. No → move on.
  3. What is the melting point? Low (below ~300°C) → simple molecular. Very high → giant covalent.
💡 The ionic fingerprint: The single most reliable sign of an ionic compound in a data table is conducting when molten/in solution but not as a solid. No other type of substance does this.

Perfect exam answers — quick reference

2 marks Define a covalent bond.

A shared pair of electrons between two non-metal atoms. (Two separate marks — must include "pair" and "between two atoms/non-metals".)

2 marks Why is methane a gas at room temperature?

Methane has weak intermolecular forces between its molecules. (1) Very little energy is needed to overcome these / its boiling point is below room temperature. (1)

2 marks Why do simple covalent substances not conduct electricity?

There are no free/mobile ions. (1) There are no free/mobile electrons — electrons are fixed in covalent bonds. (1)

3 marks Explain why propane (C₃H₈) has a higher boiling point than methane (CH₄). Both are simple molecular substances.

Propane has more electrons / is a larger molecule. (1) Therefore propane has stronger intermolecular forces between molecules. (1) More energy is needed to overcome these forces. (1)

3 marks Explain why silicon dioxide (mp 1713°C) has a much higher melting point than carbon dioxide (gas at room temperature).

SiO₂ has a giant covalent structure; CO₂ is a simple molecular structure. (1) SiO₂ has many strong covalent bonds throughout its lattice, requiring a large amount of energy to break. (1) CO₂ has only weak intermolecular forces between its molecules, requiring little energy to overcome. (1)

3 marks Write a balanced symbol equation for the complete combustion of methane (CH₄). Include state symbols.

CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g)

Mark 1: correct products (CO₂ and H₂O). Mark 2: correctly balanced (1 CH₄, 2 O₂, 1 CO₂, 2 H₂O). Mark 3: all state symbols correct — all gases at combustion temperature.

🎯 The five things every covalent bonding answer needs:
  1. Covalent bond = shared pair of electrons (not just "shared electrons")
  2. Simple molecular = weak intermolecular forces between molecules (not "weak bonds")
  3. No conductivity = no free ions AND no free electrons
  4. Diamond: 4 bonds per C, 3D network, hard, no delocalised electrons
  5. Graphite: 3 bonds per C, layers, weak forces between layers, has delocalised electrons → conducts
