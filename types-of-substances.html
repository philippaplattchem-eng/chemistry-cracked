⚙️ 1.5.6.1 · 5.1.3.1 Metals F + H ▼

Metal atoms lose their outer electrons to form positive ions surrounded by a 'sea' of delocalised electrons. This giant metallic lattice explains every property you'll be asked about.

Metallic bonding and structure

In a metal:

  • Metal atoms each lose their outer shell electrons to form positive metal ions (cations)
  • The released electrons become delocalised — they are free to move throughout the whole structure, not attached to any one atom
  • This creates a 'sea of electrons' surrounding the positive ions
  • The electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons holds the structure together — this is metallic bonding
  • The ions sit in a regular pattern called a giant metallic lattice

Giant metallic lattice: M⁺ ions in a regular arrangement, surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons

Properties of metals — and why

PropertyExplanation (link to structure)
High melting and boiling pointsStrong electrostatic forces between positive ions and delocalised electrons require a lot of energy to break
Good electrical conductorsDelocalised electrons are free to move and carry charge — works in both solid and liquid metal
Good thermal conductorsDelocalised electrons transfer kinetic energy rapidly through the structure
Malleable and ductileLayers of positive ions can slide over each other; the delocalised electrons 'flow' to maintain the bonding
ShinyDelocalised electrons at the surface reflect light
High densityIons are closely packed in a regular lattice with little empty space
⚠️ Common mistake: Saying metals conduct because they have "free electrons" gets partial credit at best. Always say delocalised electrons. Also: metals conduct electricity as solids — unlike ionic compounds, which only conduct when molten or dissolved.

Alloys

In a pure metal, all ions are the same size in neat, regular layers. These layers can slide over each other fairly easily, making pure metals relatively soft.

An alloy is a mixture of a metal with at least one other element (another metal, or a non-metal such as carbon). The different-sized atoms distort the regular lattice, so the layers cannot slide as easily — making alloys harder and stronger than the pure metal.

Alloys are harder because the different-sized atoms prevent layers from sliding over each other

Common alloys

AlloyMain componentsUses

💡 Exam tip: Never just say "alloys are harder." You must explain why: "different-sized atoms distort the regular lattice, preventing layers from sliding over each other as easily."
✅ Quick check

Explain why copper conducts electricity but sulfur does not. Refer to structure and bonding.

Copper has a giant metallic lattice with delocalised electrons that are free to move and carry charge through the structure. Sulfur is a simple molecular substance — it has no delocalised electrons and no free ions, so it cannot conduct electricity.
✅ Quick check

Titanium is a metal. Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why titanium has a high melting point.

Titanium has a giant metallic lattice structure. There are strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the positive metal ions and the delocalised electrons. A large amount of energy is required to break these forces, so titanium has a high melting point.
🔗 1.5.5.1 · 9.3 Polymers F + H ▼

A polymer is a very long chain molecule made by joining thousands of small monomer units together. For GCSE you mainly need addition polymers, which are made from monomers with a C=C double bond.

Key vocabulary

TermMeaning
MonomerSmall molecule that can join together in large numbers to form a polymer. Must have a C=C double bond for addition polymerisation.
PolymerVery large molecule made from many monomers joined together. The chain can contain thousands of carbon atoms.
Addition polymerisationMonomers with a C=C double bond join together — the double bond opens up and a single bond forms between each monomer unit. No other product is made.
Repeat unitThe smallest unit that repeats throughout the polymer chain, shown in square brackets with a subscript n.

Addition polymerisation of ethene

Ethene (CH2=CH2) is the simplest addition monomer. Under high pressure with a catalyst, the C=C double bond opens and the molecules link together to form poly(ethene).

The C=C double bond in each ethene monomer opens up to form a new single bond with the next monomer in the chain

Deducing monomer from polymer (and back)

You need to be able to draw the monomer from a given repeat unit, and draw the repeat unit from a given monomer.

Monomer → Polymer

  • Replace the C=C double bond with a C–C single bond
  • Connect the monomers end to end
  • Draw the repeat unit inside square brackets with n subscript
  • Add a bond line extending from each bracket end

Polymer → Monomer

  • Find the repeat unit inside the brackets
  • Identify the two carbons that are bonded to adjacent repeat units (at the bracket ends)
  • Replace that C–C single bond with a C=C double bond
  • Remove the bracket and the bond extensions

MonomerPolymer nameCommon uses

Propene (CH2=CHCH3)Poly(propene)Rope, food containers, textiles

Properties of polymers

Addition polymers are simple molecular substances with long carbon chains. The intermolecular forces between chains are relatively weak, so polymers tend to have low to moderate melting points compared to giant structures. They are also poor conductors of electricity (no delocalised electrons or free ions).

H Higher tier: Polymer properties depend on chain length and branching. Thermosoftening polymers (e.g. poly(ethene)) have straight or branched chains with only weak intermolecular forces between chains — they melt and can be reshaped on heating. Thermosetting polymers (e.g. Bakelite) have strong covalent crosslinks between chains — they do not melt on heating; they char or decompose instead.
⚠️ Common mistake: The repeat unit in a polymer is not the same as the monomer — the monomer has a C=C double bond, the repeat unit has only a C–C single bond at that position. Also, the n in the brackets is not the number of atoms; it's the number of repeat units in the chain (which can be thousands).
✅ Quick check

The monomer propene has the structure CH2=CHCH3. Draw the repeat unit of poly(propene) and name the type of polymerisation.

Addition polymerisation.
The repeat unit is  —[CH2—CH(CH3)]n—  (the C=C double bond becomes a single bond; the CH3 side group is kept). The structure shows two carbon atoms in the chain, each bearing the same H atoms and side groups as the monomer, but with a single bond between them and bonds extending out of the brackets.
✏️ 1.5.7.1 Representing Structures F + H ▼

There are several ways to represent the same molecule. Each one shows some things clearly and hides others. You need to know what each model is good for — and what its limitations are.

The five main representations

A common exam question (worth 6 marks) shows you five different representations of the same molecule — typically methane (CH4) — and asks you to describe what information each one gives and what its limitations are. Learn all five.

1. Molecular formula

The molecular formula (e.g. CH4) shows the number and type of each atom in the molecule — nothing more.

Limitation: Does not show how atoms are bonded, does not show the shape of the molecule, and gives no information about electron arrangement.

2. Dot-and-cross diagrams (e.g. Figure B)

Dot-and-cross diagrams show how electrons are shared (covalent bonding) or transferred (ionic bonding). One atom's electrons are drawn as dots (●) and the other atom's as crosses (✕), so you can trace where each electron came from.

Dot-and-cross diagram for water: O electrons shown as dots, H electrons as crosses; lone pairs and bonding pairs both visible

Limitation: Dot-and-cross diagrams do not show the 3D shape of a molecule or the angles between bonds. They are drawn flat on paper even though real molecules are three-dimensional.

3. Displayed (structural) formulae

A displayed formula shows every atom and every bond as a line (each line = one pair of shared electrons). It is a 2D representation — all bonds are shown but the diagram is flat. For example, ethanol:

Limitation: Does not show 3D shape or bond angles. Does not show electron arrangement. All bonds look flat on the page.

4. Ball-and-stick models

Balls represent atoms and sticks (or rods) represent covalent bonds. This model does show the 3D shape and approximate bond angles of a molecule — very useful for visualising how atoms are arranged in space.

Limitations: Ball-and-stick models do not show the relative sizes of atoms accurately — all balls tend to look similar in size. The sticks also exaggerate the distance between atom centres compared to the actual electron clouds.

5. Space-filling models

Space-filling models use overlapping spheres (sized proportional to the van der Waals radius of each atom) to show how much space the molecule actually occupies. They give the best picture of the molecule's overall shape and size.

Limitations: The bonds between atoms are hidden by the overlapping spheres, so it is hard to count bonds or see the bonding structure clearly.

💡 Exam tip: For the 6-mark extended question, work through all five representations (A–E) in order. For each one, give one thing it shows AND one limitation. The most common mark-worthy answers are:
● Molecular formula: "does not show bonding or shape"
● Dot-and-cross: "does not show the 3D shape / bond angles"
● Displayed formula: "does not show 3D shape or electron arrangement"
● Ball-and-stick: "does not show relative sizes of atoms"
● Space-filling: "does not show the individual bonds"
⚠️ Common mistake: Students say that displayed formulae "show the shape" of a molecule because they draw it at angles. They do not — the angles in a displayed formula are just for clarity on the page. The actual shape comes from the 3D arrangement of electron pairs, which is not shown.
✅ Quick check — Extended answer (6 marks)

A molecule of methane can be represented in five ways: A molecular formula (CH4), B dot-and-cross diagram, C displayed formula, D 3D ball-and-stick model, E space-filling model.

Describe what information can be obtained from each representation, including the limitations.

A — Molecular formula (CH4): Shows the number and type of each atom. Limitation: does not show bonding, shape, or electron arrangement.

B — Dot-and-cross diagram: Shows how electrons are shared in covalent bonds / shows bonding pairs and lone pairs. Limitation: does not show 3D shape or bond angles; not a realistic picture of the molecule.

C — Displayed formula: Shows all bonds and how atoms are connected. Limitation: does not show 3D shape or bond angles; does not show electron arrangement.

D — Ball-and-stick model: Shows the tetrahedral 3D shape and bond angles. Limitation: does not show relative atomic sizes; sticks do not represent real bonds.

E — Space-filling model: Shows the relative sizes of atoms and gives a realistic impression of the molecule's overall shape. Limitation: does not show individual bonds; bonds are hidden by the overlapping spheres.
✅ Quick check

State one limitation of a dot-and-cross diagram that is not a limitation of a ball-and-stick model.

A dot-and-cross diagram does not show the 3D shape or bond angles of the molecule — it is a flat 2D representation. A ball-and-stick model does show 3D shape and approximate bond angles.
