Covalent Bonding, Structures & Types of Substances

Edexcel GCSE Chemistry  ·  Topics 1.4–1.5  ·  Paper 1  ·  Spec 1.28–1.30, 1.32–1.38

1. What is a Covalent Bond?

Definition — get the exact wording right

  • A shared pair of electrons between two atoms
  • Forms between two non-metal atoms
  • Both atoms contribute one electron each to the shared pair
  • Both atoms achieve a full outer shell

Single, double and triple bonds

Single1 shared pairH–H, H–Cl, H–O
Double2 shared pairsO=O, O=C=O
Triple3 shared pairsN≡N
Exam trap: Writing "sharing electrons" alone is rejected. You must write shared pair — the mark scheme explicitly rejects answers missing the word "pair". Also reject: "transfer of electrons" (that is ionic bonding).

2. How Many Bonds Does Each Element Form?

Element Outer electrons Electrons needed (to fill shell) Bonds formed Lone pairs left Example

C / Si4440CH₄, SiCl₄
Cl / F / Br7113HCl, Cl₂
Shortcut: Bonds formed = 8 − outer electrons (for all except H, where it is 2 − 1 = 1). So N (5 outer) forms 3 bonds; O (6 outer) forms 2 bonds; Cl (7 outer) forms 1 bond.

3. Dot and Cross Diagrams — the key molecules

Rules: Use one dot + one cross in the overlap (one electron from each atom). Show outer electrons only. Add lone pairs outside the overlap on the atom that owns them.

H₂  (single bond)

1 shared pair. No lone pairs on H. Both H get full outer shell of 2.

HCl  (single bond + lone pairs)

1 shared pair. 3 lone pairs remain on Cl.

H₂O  (2 single bonds)

O forms 2 bonds. 2 lone pairs remain on O.

CH₄  (4 single bonds)

C forms 4 bonds. No lone pairs on C. (SiCl₄ is the same shape — Si has 4 outer electrons.)

NH₃  (3 single bonds + lone pair)

N forms 3 bonds. 1 lone pair remains on N.

O₂  (double bond)

O needs 2 more electrons → double bond (4 electrons in overlap). 2 lone pairs on each O.

CO₂  (2 double bonds)

C forms 2 double bonds. Each O has 2 lone pairs. C has no lone pairs.

Dot and cross mark scheme rules

  • Shared pair = one dot + one cross (not two dots or two crosses)
  • Correct number of outer electrons on each atom (1st mark)
  • Bonding pairs in overlap, lone pairs outside (2nd mark)
  • Show outer electrons only — do not draw inner shells

4. Simple Molecular Substances — Properties and Why

The key picture

Strong covalent bonds inside molecules. Weak intermolecular forces between molecules.

Properties

PropertyValue
Melting / boiling pointLOW
State at room tempOften gas or liquid
Conducts (any state)No
Soluble in waterMost are not
PropertyWhy — use this exact wording
Low melting/boiling point — often gases or liquids Only the weak intermolecular forces between molecules need to be overcome on melting/boiling. These require very little energy. (The strong covalent bonds inside the molecule are NOT broken.)
Does not conduct electricity — in any state No free/mobile ions AND no free/mobile electrons (electrons are fixed in covalent bonds). There are no charged particles that can move to carry a current.
Larger molecule = higher boiling point Larger molecules have more electrons → stronger intermolecular forces → more energy needed to separate molecules. e.g. butane > methane.
Never write: "weak bonds" or "weak covalent bonds" — the mark scheme rejects this. The covalent bonds inside each molecule are strong and are NOT broken on melting or boiling. Always write "weak intermolecular forces".

5. Diamond and Graphite — Giant Covalent Structures

Both are allotropes of carbon — same element, completely different arrangement of atoms. Both are giant covalent structures.

Diamond: 3D giant covalent lattice. Each C bonds to 4 others.
Graphite: Flat hexagonal layers. Each C bonds to 3 others. Weak forces between layers.
FeatureDiamondGraphite
BothPure carbon · Giant covalent structure · Allotropes of carbon · Very high melting point
Bonds per C4 covalent bonds → 3D network3 covalent bonds → flat hexagonal layers
Forces between layersn/a — no separate layersWeak intermolecular forces
Delocalised electrons?No — all 4 outer electrons in bondsYes — 1 per C atom (4th electron not bonded)
HardnessVery hardSoft and slippery (layers slide)
Conducts?NoYes (delocalised electrons)
Use and whyCutting tools / scalpel blades — very hard because many strong covalent bonds throughout the 3D network resist breakingElectrodes (conducts) / lubricant (layers slide) / pencil "lead" (layers leave marks)
Graphite traps: (1) Layers are held by weak intermolecular forces — NOT strong bonds. (2) Graphite also has a very high melting point (strong C–C bonds within layers must be broken). (3) Graphite is the exception to "giant covalent = insulator".

6. Silicon Dioxide (SiO₂) — and the Giant vs Simple Molecular Comparison

SiO₂: every Si bonded to 4 O atoms — continuous 3D covalent network

SiO₂ vs CO₂

Both contain only covalent bonds. SiO₂ melts at 1713°C; CO₂ is a gas at room temperature.

SiO₂CO₂
StructureGiant covalentSimple molecular
To meltBreak many strong covalent bonds throughout latticeOvercome weak intermolecular forces between molecules

Writing "SiO₂ has stronger bonds" without naming "giant covalent structure" is rejected. Must name both structures.

7. Fullerenes and Graphene

C₆₀: 60 C atoms — 20 hexagons + 12 pentagons (football shape). Each C forms 3 covalent bonds.

Key facts — all three forms

FormStructureConducts?
C₆₀Hollow sphere (discrete molecule)Limited
NanotubesHexagonal sheet rolled into cylinderYes
GrapheneSingle layer of graphite (1 atom thick)Yes
  • All are allotropes of carbon
  • Each C forms 3 covalent bonds → 1 delocalised electron per C
  • C₆₀ is a discrete molecule — held to other C₆₀ molecules by weak intermolecular forces
  • Nanotubes: extremely high tensile strength — used to reinforce composites

8. Types of Substances — Master Comparison Table

Property Ionic (e.g. NaCl) Simple molecular (e.g. CO₂) Giant covalent (e.g. diamond) Metallic (e.g. Fe)
Melting point High Low Very high Usually high
Conducts as solid? No (ions fixed) No No (except graphite) Yes
Conducts molten/solution? Yes — ions free to move No No Yes (molten)
Soluble in water? Many are Most are not No No
Classification shortcut: (1) Conducts as solid → metallic. (2) Conducts molten but not solid → ionic. (3) Low melting point, never conducts → simple molecular. (4) Very high melting point, never conducts → giant covalent.
The ionic fingerprint: Conducting when molten/dissolved but not as a solid is the unique sign of an ionic compound. No other type switches like this.

9. Exam Questions with Model Answers

Describe what is meant by a covalent bond. [2 marks]
Model Answer
  • A shared pair of electrons (1) — must say "pair", not just "shared electrons"
  • Between two non-metal atoms / electrostatic attraction between the shared electrons and the nuclei of both atoms (1)
Explain why methane (CH₄) is a gas at room temperature. [2 marks]
Model Answer
  • Methane has weak intermolecular forces between its molecules (1)
  • Very little energy is needed to overcome these / the boiling point is below room temperature (1)

Reject: "weak bonds", "weak covalent bonds" — the strong covalent bonds inside methane are NOT broken on boiling.

Explain why simple covalent substances do not conduct electricity. [2 marks]
Model Answer
  • No free / mobile ions (1)
  • No free / mobile electrons — electrons are fixed in covalent bonds / no delocalised electrons (1)

"No ions or free electrons" combined scores 1 mark only.

Propane (C₃H₈) has a higher boiling point than methane (CH₄). Both are simple molecular covalent substances. Explain why. [3 marks]
Model Answer
  • Propane has more electrons / is a larger molecule / greater relative molecular mass (1)
  • Therefore propane has stronger intermolecular forces between its molecules (1)
  • More energy is needed to overcome these intermolecular forces (1)
A glass cutter uses a diamond blade. Graphite is used as a lubricant in machine bearings. Compare the uses of diamond and graphite, explaining each in terms of bonding and structure. [6 marks]
Model Answer — must discuss both substances for more than 3 marks
  • Diamond: Giant covalent / giant lattice structure (1)
  • Each C bonded to 4 other C atoms by strong covalent bonds (1)
  • Many strong covalent bonds need a large amount of energy to break → diamond is very hard (1)
  • Therefore suitable for cutting / maintaining a sharp edge on the blade (1)
  • Graphite: Layered / hexagonal layer structure; each C bonded to 3 others within the layer (1)
  • Layers held together by weak intermolecular forces → layers slide over each other easily → soft and slippery → acts as a lubricant (1)

Reject: "strong bonds between layers" in graphite. Simply naming the structure without linking to the use.

Silicon dioxide (SiO₂) has a melting point of 1713°C. Carbon dioxide (CO₂) is a gas at room temperature. Both contain only covalent bonds. Explain why SiO₂ has a much higher melting point. [3 marks — must contrast both]
Model Answer
  • Silicon dioxide has a giant covalent structure; carbon dioxide is a simple molecular structure (1)
  • SiO₂ has many strong covalent bonds throughout the lattice, requiring a large amount of energy to break (1)
  • CO₂ has only weak intermolecular forces between molecules, requiring very little energy to overcome (1)

Reject: "stronger bonds in SiO₂" without naming giant covalent structure.

Write a balanced symbol equation for the complete combustion of methane (CH₄). Include state symbols. [3 marks]
Model Answer
  • CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g)
  • Correct products: CO₂ and H₂O (1)
  • Correctly balanced: 1 CH₄, 2 O₂, 1 CO₂, 2 H₂O (1)
  • All state symbols correct: (g) for all species at combustion temperature (1)
Element X has electronic configuration 2.5. Element Y has configuration 2.7. (i) How many bonds does X form? (ii) What is the formula of the compound formed between X and Y? [2 marks]
Model Answer
  • (i) X has 5 outer electrons → forms 3 covalent bonds (needs 3 more electrons to fill outer shell) (1)
  • (ii) XY₃ — X needs 3 bonds, Y forms 1 bond; 1 X + 3 Y → XY₃ (1)  Reject X₃Y.
