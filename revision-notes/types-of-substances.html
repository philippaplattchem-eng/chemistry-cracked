Types of Substances: Metals, Polymers & Representing Structures

Edexcel GCSE Chemistry  ·  Topics 1.5.5–1.5.7  ·  Paper 1  ·  Spec 1.5.5.1, 1.5.6.1, 5.1.3.1, 1.5.7.1

1. Metallic Bonding and Properties of Metals

The structure — use this exact language

  • Metals have a giant metallic lattice structure
  • Regular array of positive ions (M⁺) — not "metal atoms"
  • Surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons
  • Electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons holds the structure together
  • Delocalised electrons come from the outer shell of each metal atom

Key vocabulary — learn these exactly

DelocalisedFree to move throughout the whole structure
ElectrostaticForce between opposite charges (+ and −)
Giant latticeRegular, repeating 3D arrangement
M⁺ ionMetal atom that has lost its outer electron(s)
Giant metallic lattice: regular array of M⁺ ions surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons
PropertyStructural explanation — use this exact wording
High melting point Giant metallic lattice with strong electrostatic forces between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons. A large amount of energy is needed to overcome these forces.
Conducts electricity (solid and liquid) The delocalised electrons are free to move throughout the structure and carry charge. (Works in solid state — unlike ionic compounds.)
Conducts heat Delocalised electrons transfer kinetic energy quickly through the structure.
Malleable / ductile (can be shaped / drawn into wire) Layers of ions can slide over each other without breaking the metallic bonds — the delocalised electrons re-form attractions with the new layer arrangement.
Exam traps: (1) Write "positive ions" not "metal atoms" or "atoms" — atoms become ions once they lose their outer electrons. (2) Always say "electrostatic attraction" not just "attraction" or "forces". (3) Melting point questions: say "giant metallic structure" — do not just say "strong bonds".

2. Alloys

What is an alloy?

  • A mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (usually other metals or carbon)
  • The added atoms have a different size to the host metal atoms
  • This distorts the regular arrangement of ions in the lattice
  • The layers can no longer slide over each other as easily
  • Therefore alloys are harder than the pure metals they are made from

Common alloys — exam examples

AlloyMain metal(s)Use
SteelIron + carbonConstruction, cars

Exam shortcut: Every alloy hardness question wants the same three steps: (1) different-sized atoms, (2) distort the regular lattice, (3) layers cannot slide easily → harder. Learn this sequence.

3. Addition Polymerisation

Key vocabulary

MonomerSmall molecule with a C=C double bond that can join to form a polymer
PolymerVery long chain molecule made by joining many monomers together
Repeat unitThe section of the polymer chain that repeats, shown in brackets with subscript n
Addition polymerisationThe C=C double bond opens; monomers add across the double bond to form a chain

The rule: monomer → repeat unit

  • The C=C becomes C–C (double bond opens, becomes single bond)
  • All other atoms/groups stay in the same positions
  • The repeat unit is written in square brackets with n outside
  • Given the monomer → replace = with – and add the brackets
  • Given the polymer → remove the brackets and replace – with = to get the monomer
The C=C double bond (red) opens to form a C–C single bond (blue). The repeat unit is written in brackets with subscript n.
MonomerPolymer nameRepeat unit formulaUse
Ethene, CH₂=CH₂Poly(ethene) — polythene(CH₂–CH₂)nPlastic bags, bottles
Propene, CH₂=CHCH₃Poly(propene)(CH₂–CHCH₃)nRopes, food containers
Chloroethene, CH₂=CHClPoly(chloroethene) — PVC(CH₂–CHCl)nPipes, window frames
Tetrafluoroethene, CF₂=CF₂Poly(tetrafluoroethene) — PTFE(CF₂–CF₂)nNon-stick coating (Teflon)

Thermosoftening vs thermosetting polymers H

Thermosoftening (e.g. poly(ethene))

  • Long polymer chains held together by weak intermolecular forces
  • Chains can slide when heated → melts and re-sets on cooling
  • Can be recycled by melting and re-moulding

Thermosetting (e.g. Bakelite, melamine)

  • Polymer chains linked by strong covalent cross-links
  • Cross-links prevent chains sliding → does not melt, just chars
  • Cannot be recycled — the cross-links are permanent
Exam trap: You must state that the C=C double bond opens (not just "monomers join" or "bonds form"). Also: the repeat unit has the same atoms as the monomer — no atoms are lost (addition polymerisation, unlike condensation polymerisation).

4. Representing Structures

Five ways to represent a molecule, each showing different information. The exam (Q9 style) asks you to describe what each one shows AND its limitation. Learn all five for methane (CH₄).

Label Type Example (methane) What it shows Limitation
A Molecular formula CH₄ The number and type of atoms in the molecule Does not show bonding or shape
B Dot-and-cross diagram Shows C in centre, 4 H atoms with shared electron pairs (one dot + one cross in each bond) Shows how electrons are shared between atoms; which electrons come from which atom Does not show 3D shape or relative sizes of atoms
C Displayed (structural) formula H at top, bottom, left, right connected to central C by single bond lines Shows every bond as a line and how atoms are connected; all atoms shown explicitly Does not show 3D shape; bonds shown as flat 2D lines
D Ball-and-stick model 4 white H balls connected to 1 black C ball by sticks; tetrahedral 3D arrangement Shows the 3D shape (tetrahedral for CH₄) and bond angles Does not show the relative sizes of atoms (all balls look similarly sized)
E Space-filling model 4 small H spheres packed around 1 larger C sphere, overlapping to show actual relative sizes Shows the relative sizes of atoms and how they pack together in 3D Does not show individual bonds clearly; can be difficult to see internal bonding

B: Dot-and-cross (CH₄)

● = C electron   × = H electron   4 shared pairs total

C: Displayed formula (CH₄)

Quick limitation guide: A = no bonding/shape  |  B = no 3D/no sizes  |  C = no 3D shape  |  D = no relative sizes  |  E = no individual bonds. The pattern: each type gains something the previous one lacks but loses something else.

5. Exam Questions with Model Answers

Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why barium has a high melting point. [3 marks]
Model Answer
  • Barium has a giant metallic structure / giant metallic lattice (1)
  • Strong electrostatic forces between the Ba²⁺ ions and the delocalised electrons (1)
  • A large amount of energy is needed to overcome these forces / to separate the ions (1)

Reject: "strong bonds between atoms" without naming giant metallic structure. Must say "electrostatic" for the force mark.

Pure iron is too soft for many uses. Explain, in terms of structure, why steel (an alloy of iron and carbon) is harder than pure iron. [3 marks]
Model Answer
  • The carbon atoms are a different size to the iron atoms (1)
  • The carbon atoms distort the regular arrangement of ions in the lattice (1)
  • This prevents the layers of ions from sliding over each other as easily → harder (1)
Propene has the structural formula CH₂=CHCH₃. Name the polymer formed from propene monomers and draw the structural repeat unit. [2 marks]
Model Answer
  • Poly(propene) (1)
  • Repeat unit: (CH₂–CHCH₃)n — square brackets, subscript n, C=C becomes C–C, all other groups unchanged (1)

For the drawn repeat unit: brackets must be shown, the double bond must be absent (single bond only), and all substituents kept.

The diagram shows five different representations of methane, CH₄, labelled A–E: A = molecular formula, B = dot-and-cross diagram, C = displayed formula, D = ball-and-stick model, E = space-filling model. Describe what information can be obtained from each representation, including the limitations of each. [6 marks — levels-based]
Model Answer — indicative content (aim for all five representations with limitations for Level 3)
  • A (molecular formula): Shows the number and type of atoms in the molecule (1 C, 4 H). Limitation: does not show bonding or 3D shape.
  • B (dot-and-cross): Shows how electrons are shared between atoms, which electrons come from which atom, and the number of bonding pairs. Limitation: does not show 3D shape or the relative sizes of atoms.
  • C (displayed formula): Shows all the bonds as lines and how all atoms are connected to each other. Limitation: does not show the 3D shape; bonds appear flat/2D.
  • D (ball-and-stick): Shows the 3D tetrahedral shape and bond angles. Limitation: does not show the relative sizes of atoms accurately.
  • E (space-filling): Shows the relative sizes of atoms and how they are packed in 3D. Limitation: individual bonds are not clearly shown.

Level 3 (5–6 marks): All/most representations described with limitations for each.  Level 2 (3–4): Some representations with limitations.  Level 1 (1–2): One or two representations only.
