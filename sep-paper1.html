Atomic Structure & the Periodic Table
1
State the relative charge and relative mass of a proton, neutron and electron.
[3]
  • Proton: charge +1, relative mass 1
  • Neutron: charge 0, relative mass 1
  • Electron: charge −1, relative mass negligible / 1/1840 (accept "very small")
2
What is meant by the term atomic number?
[1]
  • The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom
3
Explain what is meant by the term isotope.
[2]
  • Atoms of the same element (same number of protons)
  • with different numbers of neutrons / different mass numbers
4
Write the electronic structure of: (a) sodium (Z = 11)   (b) chlorine (Z = 17).
[2]
  • (a) Sodium: 2, 8, 1
  • (b) Chlorine: 2, 8, 7
5
Explain why elements in the same group of the periodic table have similar chemical properties.
[2]
  • They have the same number of electrons in their outer shell
  • Outer shell electrons determine how an element reacts
6
CALCChlorine has two isotopes: 35Cl (75%) and 37Cl (25%). Calculate the relative atomic mass of chlorine.
[2]
  • RAM = (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) ÷ 100 [1 for correct method]
  • = 3550 ÷ 100 = 35.5 [1]
Ionic Bonding & Structure
7
Describe how an ionic bond is formed between sodium and chlorine. State what happens to the electrons.
[3]
  • Sodium loses one electron to form Na+
  • Chlorine gains one electron to form Cl−
  • Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions forms the ionic bond
8
Draw a dot-and-cross diagram to show the ionic bonding in magnesium oxide (MgO).
[3]
  • Mg shown losing 2 electrons → Mg2+ with empty outer shell
  • O shown gaining 2 electrons → O2− with full outer shell (8 electrons)
  • Charges 2+ and 2− correctly labelled; crosses from Mg shown on O
9
Sodium chloride has a giant ionic lattice structure. Explain why it has a high melting point.
[3]
  • There are many strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions
  • These forces act in all directions throughout the lattice
  • A large amount of energy is needed to overcome / break these forces
10
Explain why sodium chloride does not conduct electricity when solid but does conduct when dissolved in water or when molten.
[3]
  • When solid, ions are held in fixed positions and cannot move
  • Conductivity requires free-moving charged particles
  • When dissolved or molten, ions are free to move and carry charge
Covalent Bonding & Simple Molecular Structures
11
Describe what is meant by a covalent bond.
[2]
  • A shared pair of electrons between two atoms
  • Each atom contributes one electron to the shared pair
12
Draw a dot-and-cross diagram to show the bonding in: (a) water (H2O)   (b) ammonia (NH3).
[4]
  • H2O: oxygen shown with 2 bonding pairs and 2 lone pairs [1]; H atoms shown with full outer shell (2 electrons) [1]
  • NH3: nitrogen shown with 3 bonding pairs and 1 lone pair [1]; H atoms shown with full outer shell [1]
13
Explain why simple molecular substances such as iodine have low melting points.
[3]
  • Simple molecules have weak intermolecular forces between molecules
  • Only a small amount of energy is needed to overcome these forces
  • The strong covalent bonds within the molecules are not broken on melting
14
Explain why simple molecular substances do not conduct electricity.
[1]
  • No free electrons or ions; all electrons are involved in covalent bonds
Giant Covalent Structures
15
Compare the structures of diamond and graphite. Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not.
[5]
  • Diamond: each carbon bonded to 4 others in a tetrahedral arrangement [1]
  • Graphite: each carbon bonded to 3 others in flat layers [1]
  • Graphite has one delocalised electron per carbon (unused 4th bond) [1]
  • Delocalised electrons are free to move and carry charge → graphite conducts [1]
  • Diamond has no free electrons → cannot conduct [1]
16
Explain why diamond has a very high melting point.
[2]
  • Every carbon atom is covalently bonded to 4 others throughout the giant structure
  • A very large amount of energy is needed to break the many strong covalent bonds
17
Silicon dioxide (SiO2) is a giant covalent structure. Predict its melting point and ability to conduct electricity. Justify your answer.
[3]
  • Very high melting point [1]
  • Does not conduct electricity [1]
  • Giant covalent structure: many strong covalent bonds need large energy to break; no free electrons or ions to carry charge [1]
Metallic Bonding & Structure
18
Describe the structure and bonding in a metal, including the role of delocalised electrons.
[3]
  • Regular lattice of positive metal ions
  • Surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons
  • Strong electrostatic attraction between ions and electrons holds the structure together
19
Explain why metals are good conductors of electricity.
[2]
  • Delocalised electrons are free to move through the structure
  • Moving electrons carry charge / constitute a current
20
Explain why metals are malleable (can be shaped) but ionic compounds are brittle when a force is applied.
[4]
  • In metals, layers of ions can slide over each other when a force is applied [1]
  • Delocalised electrons move with the layers, maintaining metallic bonds throughout [1]
  • In ionic compounds, when layers shift, like charges align next to each other [1]
  • Electrostatic repulsion causes the crystal to shatter [1]
Formulae & Equations
21
Write the formula for: (a) magnesium chloride   (b) iron(III) oxide   (c) ammonium sulfate.
[3]
  • (a) MgCl2
  • (b) Fe2O3
  • (c) (NH4)2SO4
22
Balance the equation:   __ Na  +  __ O2  →  __ Na2O

What is the difference between an empirical formula and a molecular formula?
[2]

  • Molecular formula: the actual number of each type of atom in one molecule
Moles & Calculations
24
CALCCalculate the Mr of calcium carbonate, CaCO3. (Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16)
[1]
  • 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100
25
CALCCalculate the number of moles in 11 g of CO2. (Mr = 44)
[2]
  • moles = mass ÷ Mr = 11 ÷ 44 [1 for method]
  • = 0.25 mol [1]
26
CALCWhat mass of magnesium oxide (MgO) is produced when 12 g of magnesium burns completely? (Mg = 24, O = 16)
[3]
  • Equation: 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO; moles of Mg = 12 ÷ 24 = 0.5 mol [1]
  • 1:1 molar ratio → moles of MgO = 0.5 mol [1]
  • Mass = 0.5 × 40 = 20 g [1]
27
Define percentage yield and state one reason why it is rarely 100%.
[3]
  • % yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100 [1]
  • Rarely 100% because: reversible reactions don't go to completion [1]
  • Accept: product lost during transfer / filtration / side reactions occur [1]
28
CALCIn the reaction N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3, calculate the atom economy for producing NH3. (N = 14, H = 1)
[3]
  • Atom economy = (Mr of desired products ÷ total Mr of all products) × 100 [1 for formula]
  • Mr of 2NH3 = 2 × 17 = 34; only product is NH3 [1]
  • = 34 ÷ 34 × 100 = 100% [1]
29
State one advantage of a high atom economy for industry.
[1]
  • Less waste produced / more sustainable / cheaper (any one)

States of Matter
1
Describe the arrangement and movement of particles in a solid.
[2]
  • Particles are closely packed in a regular arrangement
  • Particles vibrate about fixed positions
2
Explain, using particle theory, why gases can be compressed but liquids cannot.
[2]
  • In gases, particles are far apart with large spaces, so can be pushed closer together
  • In liquids, particles are already close together with little space between them
3
A substance melts sharply at 156 °C. What does this suggest about the purity of the substance?
[1]
  • The substance is pure (no impurities present)
4
How does the presence of impurities affect the melting point and boiling point of a substance?
[2]
  • Impurities lower the melting point and raise the boiling point
  • Melting / boiling occurs over a range of temperatures rather than at a fixed point
Separation Techniques
5
Describe how you would separate a mixture of sand and salt.
[4]
  • Add water to dissolve the salt (sand does not dissolve) [1]
  • Filter to separate sand (residue) from salt solution (filtrate) [1]
  • Evaporate the filtrate to concentrate the solution [1]
  • Leave to crystallise; filter and dry the crystals [1]
6
Explain how simple distillation is used to obtain pure water from salt water.
[3]
  • Heat the salt water; water evaporates and the steam/vapour rises [1]
  • Steam passes through a condenser and cools back to liquid water [1]
  • Pure water collected in a separate flask; salt remains [1]
7
Explain why fractional distillation, rather than simple distillation, is used to separate ethanol and water.
[2]
  • Ethanol and water have similar / close boiling points (78 °C and 100 °C)
  • A fractionating column maintains a temperature gradient, allowing effective separation of liquids with close boiling points
8
Describe how paper chromatography works to separate a mixture of dyes.
[4]
  • Sample placed as a spot on the baseline of chromatography paper [1]
  • Paper placed in solvent below the baseline; solvent moves up by capillary action [1]
  • Components travel at different rates depending on solubility in solvent and attraction to paper [1]
  • Components separate into distinct spots at different heights [1]
9
CALCIn a chromatography experiment, the solvent front travels 9.0 cm and a spot travels 6.3 cm. Calculate the Rf value.
[2]
  • Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent front [1 for method]
  • = 6.3 ÷ 9.0 = 0.70 [1]
10
A student compares the Rf value of a food dye to a reference sample and the values match. What conclusion can the student draw?
[1]
  • The food dye is likely to be the same substance as the reference sample (accept "may contain the same compound")
Water Treatment
11
State the three main stages used to make water safe to drink.
[3]
  • Sedimentation: chemicals added to make particles clump and settle [1]
  • Filtration: through sand / gravel to remove remaining particles [1]
  • Chlorination: chlorine added to kill bacteria / microorganisms [1]
12
Explain why water is treated with chlorine. State one risk associated with chlorination.
[2]
  • Kills bacteria / microorganisms that could cause disease [1]
  • Risk: chlorine can react with organic compounds to form toxic / potentially carcinogenic compounds [1]

Oxidation & Reduction
4
Define oxidation and reduction in terms of electron transfer.
[2]
  • Oxidation: loss of electrons
  • Reduction: gain of electrons (OIL RIG)
5
In the reaction CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O, identify which substance is oxidised and which is reduced. Explain your answer.
[4]

  • H2 is oxidised: hydrogen loses electrons / gains oxygen [1]
  • This is a redox reaction (oxidation and reduction occurring simultaneously) [1]

Electrolysis
11
Define the term electrolyte.
[1]

12
State the products at each electrode when molten lead bromide is electrolysed.
[2]
  • Cathode (−): lead
  • Anode (+): bromine
13
State the products at each electrode when dilute sulfuric acid is electrolysed using inert electrodes.
[2]
  • Cathode: hydrogen
  • Anode: oxygen
14
State the products at each electrode when concentrated sodium chloride solution is electrolysed. Explain why chlorine is produced at the anode rather than oxygen.

Metal Extraction, Alloys & LCA
1
Explain what is meant by a life cycle assessment (LCA).
[2]
  • A method to assess the total environmental impact of a product
  • Over its entire life from raw material extraction to disposal
2
State four stages considered in an LCA.
[4]
  • Extraction and processing of raw materials [1]
  • Manufacturing and packaging [1]
  • Use of the product [1]
  • Disposal / end of life [1]
3
Give one limitation of using an LCA to compare two products.
[1]
  • Some data is difficult to quantify / involves value judgements (accept: data may be incomplete / biased; hard to compare different types of environmental impact)
4
Give two environmental and one economic reason why metals should be recycled.
[3]
  • Environmental: reduces mining and associated habitat destruction [1]
  • Environmental: reduces energy use and CO2 emissions compared to extraction [1]
  • Economic: cheaper than extracting from ore / metal ores are finite [1]
5
Define the term alloy. Give one example and state its component metals.
[3]
  • A mixture of a metal with one or more other elements [1]
  • Example: steel [1]; components: iron + carbon [1] (accept brass = Cu + Zn; bronze = Cu + Sn; stainless steel = Fe + Cr/Ni)
6
Explain, using ideas about structure, why alloys are harder than the pure metals they contain.
[3]
  • In pure metals, all atoms are the same size in regular layers that can slide over each other [1]
  • In an alloy, atoms of different sizes disrupt the regular arrangement [1]
  • This prevents layers from sliding, making the alloy harder [1]
Dynamic Equilibrium
7
Explain what is meant by a dynamic equilibrium.
[3]
  • A reversible reaction in a closed system [1]
  • Where forward and reverse reactions occur at equal rates [1]
  • So the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant [1]
8
State Le Chatelier's principle.
[2]
  • If a change is imposed on a system at equilibrium, the equilibrium will shift to oppose that change
  • (2 marks for a complete accurate statement; 1 mark for a partial statement)
9
For the reaction: N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g)   ΔH = −92 kJ/mol, predict and explain the effect on the equilibrium position of: (a) increasing temperature   (b) increasing pressure   (c) removing ammonia as it forms.
[6]
  • (a) Shifts left [1]; reverse reaction is endothermic, absorbs extra heat; less NH3 produced [1]
  • (b) Shifts right [1]; 4 moles gas on left → 2 moles on right; shifting right reduces gas molecules / pressure [1]
  • (c) Shifts right [1]; to replace the removed ammonia [1]
The Haber Process
10
State the raw materials used in the Haber process and where they come from.
[2]
  • Nitrogen from the air / fractional distillation of liquid air [1]
  • Hydrogen from natural gas (methane) / cracking of hydrocarbons [1]
11
State the conditions used in the Haber process (temperature, pressure, catalyst).
[3]
  • Temperature: approximately 450 °C
  • Pressure: approximately 200 atm
  • Catalyst: iron
12
Explain why a compromise temperature of around 450 °C is used rather than a very high or very low temperature.
[4]
  • High temperature increases rate of reaction [1]
  • But shifts equilibrium left, giving a lower yield of NH3 [1]
  • Low temperature gives higher yield but the rate is too slow to be economical [1]
  • 450 °C is a compromise giving acceptable yield at acceptable rate [1]
13
What is the role of the iron catalyst? Explain why using a catalyst does not change the equilibrium position.
[3]
  • Increases the rate at which equilibrium is reached [1]
  • Speeds up both forward and reverse reactions equally [1]
  • Does not change the position of equilibrium or the yield [1]
14
In the Haber process, unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled. Suggest why this is done.
[2]
  • To increase the overall yield of ammonia [1]
  • To reduce waste and cost / increase efficiency [1]

Corrosion & Rusting
4
State the two substances needed for iron to rust.
[2]
  • Water (or moisture) [1] and oxygen [1]
5
Explain why galvanising (coating with zinc) protects iron more effectively than painting.
[3]
  • Paint can be scratched or chipped, exposing iron to water and oxygen [1]
  • Zinc also acts as a sacrificial metal [1]

[3]
  • A more reactive metal is attached to the iron/steel structure [1]
  • The more reactive metal corrodes / oxidises in preference to the iron [1]
  • Example: zinc blocks on ship hulls / magnesium rods on underground pipelines [1]
